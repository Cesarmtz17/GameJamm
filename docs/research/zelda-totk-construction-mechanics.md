# Per-part physics turns joins into machines

**Public sources do not document the rules that decide whether two parts join, how many can join, or what weight and stability limits apply.** What developers did publish is the architecture: every object in Tears of the Kingdom (TOTK) is physics-driven, each object carries its own properties, and motion comes from mass, velocity and constraints rather than from per-vehicle code ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/how-nintendo-did-the-impossible-with-tears-of-the-kingdom-s-physics-system); [Shacknews GDC recap](https://www.shacknews.com/article/139210/zelda-totk-gdc-2024-panel-recap)). Three verbs sit on top of that model. Ultrahand joins and moves objects, Fuse merges a weapon or shield with a material, and Autobuild rebuilds saved schematics from inventory ([GameSpot Autobuild guide](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/zelda-tears-of-the-kingdom-autobuild-guide/1100-6514038/); [Video Games SI Fuse guide](https://videogames.si.com/guides/zelda-tears-of-the-kingdom-fuse)). Powered Zonai devices such as fans, wheels and rockets draw on Zonai charges, but the sources disagree on consumption rates. For a natural-language-to-structure DSL, the defensible design is a closed set of typed components with declarative per-part properties, plus a validation layer. The join rules and limits would have to be defined by the DSL author, because no game source supplies them. Claims below are tagged **[single-source]**, **[snippet-only]** (seen only in a search summary, page not read) or **[inference]**.

## Parts are catalogued by function, but the numbers rest on one table

The best device catalog is a Kiranico database listing roughly 27 Zonai devices with a one-line function and a "MaxLife" value. It is the only readable source for these function descriptions, so every row is **[single-source]**. Its entries show a clear pattern: most devices share a MaxLife of **54,000**, while fragile parts are far lower, such as the Rocket at **90** and the Spring at **500** ([Kiranico Zonai devices](https://zelda.kiranico.com/totk/data/zonai_devices)). Reading MaxLife as durability is an **[inference]**, since the source does not say so.

| Part | Function (per Kiranico) | MaxLife |
|---|---|---|
| Fan | Internal propeller makes wind for moving objects and thrust | 54,000 |
| Wing | Harnesses lift; centered balance helps distance | 2,250 |
| Rocket | Strong thrust, burns out quickly | 90 |
| Small / Big Wheel | Powered; small is fast on flat ground, big handles rough terrain and shallow water | 54,000 each |
| Battery / Big Battery | Temporary power for other devices / single use, several times a normal battery | 4,500 / 63,000 |
| Steering Stick | Steers an attached conveyance and activates all connected devices at once | 54,000 |
| Stabilizer | Stands upright when activated; secures footing | 54,000 |
| Balloon | Rises with hot air; hotter air rises faster | 3,600 |
| Spring | Compresses, then bounces objects away | 500 |
| Time Bomb | Explodes on a timer; the blast can trigger other devices | 240 |
| Cannon | Fires explosive shots at intervals | 54,000 |
| Portable Pot | Cooks one recipe anywhere; works best on flat surfaces | not listed |

The same table covers Hover Stone, Cart, Sled, Stake, Mirror and several emitters ([Kiranico Zonai devices](https://zelda.kiranico.com/totk/data/zonai_devices)). Power is the one mechanic that crosses the whole catalog. Zonai charges "are essential for powering Zonai devices," and soldier, captain and flux constructs drop them ([Pocket Tactics](https://www.pockettactics.com/zelda-tears-of-the-kingdom/zonai)). That article does not give per-device draw or a charge-to-energy conversion. A search summary says all devices cost **3 Crystallized Charges** except big batteries at 30, but that figure is **[snippet-only]** and unverified ([Prima Games search summary](https://primagames.com/gaming/tears-of-the-kingdom-how-to-use-zonai-charge)). Claims that a fan drains much faster than a wheel, and that powered devices run about 30 minutes, come from low-authority guides and are unconfirmed.

Prebuilt schematics show how parts combine. The Excavator is listed as a Board, three Big Wheels, a Steering Stick, a Stabilizer and Spikes, and the All-Purpose Raft as two Boards, six Logs, a Sail and a Portable Pot ([Zelda Dungeon Excavator](https://zeldadungeon.net/wiki/Excavator); [Zelda Dungeon All-Purpose Raft](https://zeldadungeon.net/wiki/All-Purpose_Raft)). Both pages are stubs seen only as summaries, so these lists are **[snippet-only]** and may be incomplete. Board, Log and Sail are materials, not devices, and only the devices carry the power mechanic.

## Joining is physics between rigid bodies, and the rule set is undocumented

The developer-level account is the strongest evidence in this topic. The team set out to let players fuse objects into vehicles, planning "a vast library of objects that would each contain autonomous properties," and it expected fusing any two objects to be "very, very difficult" but committed to it anyway ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/how-nintendo-did-the-impossible-with-tears-of-the-kingdom-s-physics-system)). Joints are real objects in that design. Suspension uses "a range limit constraint and a clean constraint," and the Portable Pot gets a joint at its base so it stays level on uneven ground ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/how-nintendo-did-the-impossible-with-tears-of-the-kingdom-s-physics-system)). A wheel is three rigid bodies, wheel, motor and shaft, and torque transfers from the motor through the shaft to produce forward motion through ground friction ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/how-nintendo-did-the-impossible-with-tears-of-the-kingdom-s-physics-system)). Mass and movement are "calculated automatically from material and shape," then tuned by hand, with boards and plates made thicker than real ones ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/how-nintendo-did-the-impossible-with-tears-of-the-kingdom-s-physics-system)). Water resistance uses the object's projected area in its direction of motion, not a uniform velocity decay ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/how-nintendo-did-the-impossible-with-tears-of-the-kingdom-s-physics-system)).

The engine is less settled. Havok lists TOTK on its "Havok Powered" page, which is a Havok listing, not a Nintendo statement ([Havok Powered](https://www.havok.com/havok-powered/)). Digital Trends says the game uses Havok as a foundation with Nintendo's own physics on top, but the claim is unattributed writer narration ([Digital Trends](https://digitaltrends.com/computing/how-nintendo-designed-tears-of-the-kingdom-physics)). Treat "Havok plus a Nintendo layer" as **[single-source]** writer narration, not a developer statement.

Visible glue is the one attachment cue developers discussed. Aonuma noted the "glue bulging out" between attached objects, and Takizawa defended it as the clearest way to show what is attached. The same attach sound plays whatever is joined ([Nintendo Ask the Developer](https://www.nintendo.com/sg/interview/totk/04.html)). The glue mark also carried into sound design ([GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/tears-of-the-kingdom-dev-describes-utterly-chaotic-ultrahand-development-it-broke-it-went-flying), **[snippet-only]**).

What the sources do not give is the actual rule for Ultrahand. None of the readable sources describe attach or detach conditions, surface connection rules, joint type taxonomy, weight thresholds or stability limits. The GameSpot Ultrahand guide was not readable ([GameSpot Ultrahand guide](https://www.gamespot.com/amp-articles/zelda-tears-of-the-kingdom-ultrahand-guide/1100-6514037/)). Fuse adds a little more. Fuse makes a hybrid that keeps traits of both parts, with the example of a boulder fused to a tree branch as a stronger hammer-like weapon ([Twinfinite](https://twinfinite.net/2023/03/tears-of-the-kingdoms-new-fuse-mechanic-fixes-breath-of-the-wilds-biggest-problem/), **[snippet-only]**). Detaching a fused part lets you fuse a new one ([GameSkinny](https://www.gameskinny.com/?p=86560), **[snippet-only]**). Fuse Recycling applies to specific weapons, per a single ggrecon summary ([ggrecon](https://ggrecon.com/guides/zelda-tears-of-the-kingdom-fuse-recycling/), **[single-source]**). The developers' note that they made "special adjustments to each item" for fused weapons suggests weapons are tuned individually, not governed by one generic rule ([Nintendo Ask the Developer](https://www.nintendo.com/sg/interview/totk/04.html)).

Autobuild is a rebuild-from-recipe tool. It is the fifth and last ability on Link's Zonai arm, and it is opened by holding L and selecting it from the menu, where learned schematics and a history of builds appear ([GameSpot Autobuild guide](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/zelda-tears-of-the-kingdom-autobuild-guide/1100-6514038/), **[snippet-only]**). Saving Ultrahand recipes requires it ([GameWith](https://gamewith.net/zelda-totk/article/show/38843), **[snippet-only]**). Per a guide summary, it recreates builds by making missing parts from inventory materials, and some builds need Zonai charges to power the machinery ([Game Guides](https://www.gamerguides.com/the-legend-of-zelda-tears-of-the-kingdom/guide/building/autobuild-recipes), **[snippet-only]**). Whether it repairs damaged builds is **unconfirmed**. The unlock path also conflicts across sources, one naming Josha at Lookout Landing and another the Steward Construct in the Great Abandoned Central Mine ([Zelda Wiki Autobuild](https://zelda.fandom.com/wiki/Autobuild), **[snippet-only]**). Autobuild being a rebuild tool is an **[inference]** from those summaries.

## Designed systems and player-made machines are separate layers

The developers describe a deliberate split. The designed layer is the physics model, per-object tuning, joints, the attach cue, the weapon adjustments, and the decision that "everything" is physics-driven, which technical director Takuhiro Dohta framed as "multiplicative gameplay" ([The Verge via mirror](https://hn.jakechampion.name/reader/https://www.theverge.com/24115898/tears-of-the-kingdom-physics-gameplay-design-gdc-2024), **[single-source]** secondary write-up). Takayama is quoted there as saying that "everything, without exception being physics-driven, is necessary to make multiplicative gameplay a reality" ([The Verge via mirror](https://hn.jakechampion.name/reader/https://www.theverge.com/24115898/tears-of-the-kingdom-physics-gameplay-design-gdc-2024)). Dohta's line that the team wanted "a system that makes fun things happen" rather than "something fun" is the closest developer statement to the idea of emergence, reported in Digital Trends ([Digital Trends](https://digitaltrends.com/computing/how-nintendo-designed-tears-of-the-kingdom-physics)). The word "emergent" does not appear in any developer quote found. Where the analogy notes say "emergent solutions," that is the writer's wording, not a developer's.

The unplanned layer is the one players built. The team's own prototypes were "using only the mechanisms and parts available in the previous game," and included a car with cog tires, a paddle steamer and a cannon-on-car tank ([Nintendo Ask the Developer](https://www.nintendo.com/sg/interview/totk/04.html)). Dohta's prototype was a log with tires that "moved forward," and Aonuma's stated design intent was that attaching two long things makes something longer ([Nintendo Ask the Developer](https://www.nintendo.com/sg/interview/totk/04.html)). The developers said heavy tuning was needed "because of the sheer number of variations," and that Dohta wanted players to "find their favorite" ([Nintendo Ask the Developer](https://www.nintendo.com/sg/interview/totk/04.html)). Leads said they were "blown away" by player builds ([GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/zelda-leads-were-blown-away-by-what-tears-of-the-kingdom-players-made-with-ultrahand-its-beyond-even-the-development-teams-imagination), **[snippet-only]**). The Portable Pot became a reused joint in many player vehicles and robots, which the notes classify as unplanned ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/how-nintendo-did-the-impossible-with-tears-of-the-kingdom-s-physics-system)). Whether the Pot's vehicle use was planned is an **[inference]**. Sources do not say which specific builds were intended and which were not.

Scripted behavior does exist at the device level, which is where the boundary is fuzzy. The Steering Stick "activates all connected devices at once," and the Stabilizer "stands upright when activated," both per Kiranico ([Kiranico Zonai devices](https://zelda.kiranico.com/totk/data/zonai_devices)). Those are device-specific triggers layered on top of physics. The gears case shows where scripts broke. Gates and cogs were originally non-physics objects, so they clipped with physics objects; gears passed through a metal box until the box was made physics-driven ([Shacknews GDC recap](https://www.shacknews.com/article/139210/zelda-totk-gdc-2024-panel-recap), **[snippet-only]**; [The Verge via mirror](https://hn.jakechampion.name/reader/https://www.theverge.com/24115898/tears-of-the-kingdom-physics-gameplay-design-gdc-2024), **[single-source]**). Takayama said the clash between non-physics objects and Ultrahand's freedom caused "daily problems," and that the fix was uniform physics rather than a rule per exploit ([Shacknews GDC recap](https://www.shacknews.com/article/139210/zelda-totk-gdc-2024-panel-recap), **[snippet-only]**). The **[inference]** is that a device's trigger is scripted while its motion, mass and collision are physics. The sources do not state that split directly.

## Design lessons for a natural-language construction DSL

The TOTK evidence favors one generic physics layer and a small set of composable component types, not per-item scripts. The sources say the team "created a system in which vehicles could be made" instead of a vehicle program, and that bridges, wagons and sound came from component-level systems ([The Verge via mirror](https://hn.jakechampion.name/reader/https://www.theverge.com/24115898/tears-of-the-kingdom-physics-gameplay-design-gdc-2024), **[single-source]**). For a DSL, that suggests each component type declares a short behavior contract (mass, joint types, interactions) that the engine applies uniformly. Sockets and typed joints are a design proposal, not a documented pattern. No source found documents socket schemas in TOTK, Besiege, Scrap Mechanic, Stormworks or KSP.

Validation has a better precedent in LLM content generation. One arXiv study has the model emit a custom JSON DSL, which a deterministic backend executes after checking syntax, component types and parameter ranges, with missing parameters defaulted and bad output falling back to a harmless effect ([arXiv 2510.16952](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.16952v1)). The same paper warns that error handling "salvaged most flawed outputs," so pass/fail metrics hide errors, and that adversarial inputs were not tested ([arXiv 2510.16952](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.16952v1)). A reasonable stack is a closed component list, a typed graph of parts and connections with reference-integrity checks (an **[inference]**), clamped parameters, and a safe default. Budget and part-count caps have only weak precedent, from community tournament rules in Besiege and Stormworks ([Besiege Bots rules](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/19loFSAuqFSxgLp28y1vSREPj58C0LOPUbf1n_6Un-6w/edit), community document). Shape grammars are the academic reference for rule-based construction, but no source applies them to physics-simulated builds ([Waterloo CS-2009-23A](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/research/tr/2009/CS-2009-23A.pdf), **not fetched**).

## Limits, edge cases and what a player test must settle

The publicly documented failures cluster around the attached state and the physics engine. The most discussed is **Fuse Entanglement**, where an item acts as if fused to Link's shield without visibly being fused, set up by pressing ZL late. It enables the Pocket Rocket and the duplication of equipment ([Zelda Speedruns](https://www.zeldaspeedruns.com/totk/tech/fuse-entanglement), **[single-source]** community page). That shows the attached flag and the physical state can diverge, which is an **[inference]** and not a developer claim. Pocket Rocket behavior changed by version: on 1.1.x the rocket can be consumed if left out too long, unlike 1.0 ([Zelda Speedruns](https://www.zeldaspeedruns.com/totk/tech/fuse-entanglement), **[single-source]**). The Zelda Wiki snippet says Fuse Entanglement is not patched and underlies many glitches, with one related glitch fixed in 1.2.0 ([Zelda Wiki glitches](https://zeldawiki.wiki/wiki/Glitches_in_Tears_of_the_Kingdom), **[snippet-only]**). Calculated weights were "too heavy" for some shapes and were adjusted by hand, and kinematic objects broke under physics ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/how-nintendo-did-the-impossible-with-tears-of-the-kingdom-s-physics-system)). A guide claims each added part weighs down fans and drains the Energy Cell, and smaller builds perform better, but it is a community claim ([gamertagmythras guide](https://gamertagmythras.com/blog/zelda-tears-of-the-kingdom/totk-ultrahand-zonai-devices-guide), **[snippet-only]**).

The gaps matter for the DSL, so each should be settled by a player test, which is play and not file extraction. Attach rules can be tested by fusing every pair of a small part set and recording which pairs hold, and whether surface placement matters. A fuse or part limit can be found by stacking parts until the build fails, and a weight limit by adding boards until a wheeled cart stops moving at the same speed. Stability can be tested by building tall stacks on and off a Stabilizer. Charge consumption is the one with contradictory sources, and a timed run is the test: start a fan and a wheel from a full battery under identical load, and count charges until each stops. Autobuild repair can be tested by damaging a built vehicle and then running Autobuild to see whether the missing parts come back or the whole build is rebuilt. Emergence versus scripting can be probed by building the same parts in a different order and checking whether the result matches. If it does, the build behaves like a pure physics composition, and any difference points to a device-level script.

## Conclusion

The useful finding is architectural, not a rulebook. TOTK's developers made every object physics-driven and gave each object its own properties, then left the combinations to players. That makes the game's machines a consequence of a few generic rules rather than of hand-scripted vehicles, which supports a DSL built the same way. The rules that would make such a DSL precise, namely attach conditions, joint types, weight and stability limits, and per-device charge costs, are not public. The DSL author has to define them, and the player tests above are the cheapest way to pin down the values that matter.
